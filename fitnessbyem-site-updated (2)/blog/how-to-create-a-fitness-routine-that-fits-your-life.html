How to Create a Fitness Routine That Actually Fits Your Life

Emily Gray , Masters Graduate Exercise Physiologist
6 July 20265 min readWeight Loss

The most common mistake I see women make when trying to build a fitness routine isn't choosing the wrong program. It's building the routine for the wrong person.

They build it for their ideal self. The version of themselves with unlimited time, consistent energy, no competing demands, and a schedule that never changes. Then they're surprised when real life — with its meetings and school pickups and exhausted Thursdays — makes the routine impossible to maintain.

The best fitness routine isn't the most comprehensive one. It's the one you can actually repeat, week after week, including the hard weeks.

Here's how to build it.

Start With Your Real Life, Not Your Ideal Life

Before you write a single session into your calendar, I want you to do something most fitness plans never ask you to do: look at your actual week.

Not the week you wish you had. The week you actually have.

  • What days are genuinely available for training — not in theory, but in reality?
  • What time of day do you have the most energy and the fewest obligations?
  • Which weeks are reliably busier — the end of the month, school holidays, certain work cycles?
  • What happens to your schedule when things go wrong — a sick kid, a big deadline, a rough night's sleep?

Your routine needs to work on that last question. Not just on the good weeks. Because the good weeks will take care of themselves. What determines your long-term results is what you do on the hard weeks.

Build your routine around your real life — and then build in contingency for when even that gets disrupted.

How Many Sessions Per Week Should You Actually Commit To?

The honest answer: fewer than you think.

Most women come to me wanting to train five or six days a week. When I ask them how many days they've actually trained consistently in the last month, it's usually two or three.

There's a significant gap between aspiration and reality — and building a routine that lives in aspiration is one of the fastest ways to stay in the start-stop cycle.

My recommendation for most women building a sustainable routine:

  • If you're starting out or returning after a break: three days per week, full body sessions, 45–60 minutes each
  • If you have an established baseline of consistency: four days per week, upper/lower split
  • If life is genuinely hectic right now: two sessions is a minimum — it's enough to maintain and enough to stay in the game

Commit to a number that you can hit on your worst week — not your best.

Build Around Compound Movements, Not Complexity

One of the biggest reasons routines fall apart is that they're too complicated to execute when energy is low.

When your schedule is full and your motivation is depleted, you don't want to be standing in the gym trying to remember which machine comes next or what superset you're supposed to do. You want something familiar, simple, and repeatable.

Build your routine around compound movements you can do on autopilot:

  • Lower body: Squats, Romanian deadlifts, hip thrusts, lunges
  • Upper body push: Bench press, shoulder press, push-ups
  • Upper body pull: Rows, lat pulldowns, face pulls
  • Core: Planks, deadbugs, carries

These six movement patterns cover your entire body, drive the most meaningful physiological adaptation, and can be executed regardless of which equipment is available.

Keep the structure consistent. Change the variables — weight, reps, tempo — as you progress. But keep the skeleton the same. Familiarity is underrated.

Keep Nutrition Simple Enough to Repeat

Nutrition is where most routines fall apart — not because the strategy is wrong, but because it's unsustainable.

Meal prepping six different recipes every Sunday and tracking every gram of food might work for two weeks. It won't work for six months. And six months is where results actually live.

A realistic nutrition approach for a busy life looks like this:

  • Protein at every meal — this is the non-negotiable. Everything else is secondary.
  • Three to five meals or snacks you rotate regularly — familiar, easy to prepare, protein-rich
  • Flexible eating at social events — one dinner out doesn't derail anything. Stress and guilt about one dinner out might.
  • No foods off limits — restriction creates obsession. Flexibility creates sustainability.

You don't need a perfect meal plan. You need a reliable structure that doesn't require heroic effort to maintain.

Plan for Disruption Before It Happens

This is the step most people skip — and it's the reason most routines eventually collapse.

Disruption is not the exception. It is the rule. If your routine has no plan for when things go sideways, it will go sideways and never come back.

Before you start, decide:

What happens when you miss a session? You move on and do your next scheduled session. There is no making it up. There is no extending the week. Next session, as planned.

What happens when you can't get to the gym? You have a backup. A 20-minute bodyweight session at home. A walk. Something — anything — that keeps the habit alive.

What happens during a genuinely brutal week? You invoke your minimum baseline. Two sessions. Some daily movement. That's enough to stay in the game.

What happens on holidays or travel? You decide in advance what your minimum looks like — and you hold to it without guilt. Rest is part of the plan.

Planning for disruption in advance means disruption doesn't derail you. It just triggers the backup plan.

The Routine That Gets Results Isn't the Hardest One

I want to close with something that runs counter to most of what the fitness industry sells.

The routine that gets you results is not the most intense one. It's not the most comprehensive one. It's not the one with the most sessions or the strictest nutrition rules.

It's the one you can repeat. Week after week, month after month — on good weeks and hard weeks alike.

Consistency is what beats perfection every single time — not because effort doesn't matter, but because sustained, consistent effort over time compounds in ways that short bursts of intensity never can.

Build a routine for the life you actually have. Commit to your minimum. Show up when it's inconvenient. And give it the time it needs to work.
