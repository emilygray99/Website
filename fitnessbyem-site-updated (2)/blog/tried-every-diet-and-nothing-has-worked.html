Tried Everything and Nothing Has Worked? Here's Why — And What to Do Instead

Emily Gray , Masters Graduate Exercise Physiologist
6 April 20266 min readMindset

You've done the 12-week challenge. You've tried keto, low-carb, 1200 calories, intermittent fasting. You've had gym memberships you've used for a month and then quietly let lapse. You've meal prepped on Sundays with genuine enthusiasm and been eating cereal for dinner by Wednesday.

And through all of it, the results have either not come, not lasted, or come at a cost that wasn't sustainable — your social life, your relationship with food, your sanity.

If this is your history, I need you to hear something clearly: you are not broken. You are not lazy. You do not have a broken metabolism or exceptional bad luck.

You've been using approaches that aren't designed to work long-term. And that's a very different problem — because it means the solution isn't trying harder. It's trying differently.

Why Nothing Has Stuck: The Real Reasons

The Approach Was Built for a Sprint, Not a Marathon

Most diet and fitness programs are designed to produce fast results. A dramatic transformation in 6 or 12 weeks. Before and after photos. A number on the scale that moves quickly enough to feel like something is happening.

The problem is that fast results almost always require unsustainable measures — extreme calorie restriction, elimination of entire food groups, training volumes that aren't maintainable around real life. The results are real. But they exist only while you're on the program. The moment you return to normal life, the results disappear with the structure that created them.

This isn't a failure of your willpower. It's a design flaw in the program.

The Approach Didn't Fit Your Life

One of the most common patterns I see: women who follow a program perfectly for two or three weeks and then life interrupts — a work trip, a family event, a sick child, a genuinely bad week — and the program has no flexibility to accommodate it.

Because it wasn't built for real life. It was built for ideal conditions. And when ideal conditions end, the program ends with them.

A sustainable approach bends. It has room for the dinner out and the week where you only made it to the gym twice. It continues rather than collapsing the moment something goes wrong.

You Were Under-eating and Overstressing Your Body

This is counterintuitive, but important: eating too little is one of the most reliable ways to stall fat loss.

Chronic under-eating — which is extremely common in women who've been on multiple restrictive diets — signals to your body that food is scarce. In response, your body downregulates your metabolism, becomes more efficient at storing fat, and ramps up hunger hormones. You're fighting your own biology.

Add in the cortisol spike that comes from heavy training and the stress of trying to be perfect, and you've created conditions that actively work against fat loss — even when you're doing everything "right" by conventional diet culture standards.

The Mindset Was All-or-Nothing

All-or-nothing thinking is perhaps the most significant barrier to lasting results I encounter. It sounds like:

  • "I've already had one bad meal, I've ruined the day"
  • "I missed two sessions this week, I may as well start over"
  • "If I can't do it perfectly, there's no point doing it at all"

This mindset doesn't just make things harder psychologically — it creates a pattern where any imperfection triggers a full reset, which means the effort never compounds. You're always starting from scratch.

What Actually Works (And Why It Feels Different)

Flexible Nutrition Over Rigid Restriction

The approach that works long-term isn't one that eliminates foods or demands perfection every day. It's one built around flexibility — understanding how to eat in a way that supports your goals without making food feel like the enemy.

This means prioritising protein, eating whole foods most of the time, and having genuine room for the foods you enjoy without guilt. Not because indulgence doesn't matter, but because deprivation creates obsession — and obsession is what causes binges, guilt cycles, and ultimately, giving up.

No food should feel forbidden. The moment something is forbidden, your brain wants it more.

Strength Training as the Foundation

If you've been doing mostly cardio and wondering why your body composition isn't changing, this is your answer: cardio burns calories during exercise. Strength training changes what your body looks like and how efficiently it burns calories at rest.

Building lean muscle mass increases your resting metabolic rate. That means your body burns more calories around the clock — not just during your 45-minute session. It's the single most effective long-term fat loss tool available to women, and it's chronically underused.

Habits Over Heroics

Lasting results don't come from dramatic effort over short periods. They come from boring, consistent habits maintained over long ones.

The women who transform their bodies — and keep those results — aren't training twice a day or eating chicken and rice six times. They're training three or four times a week, eating in a way that feels sustainable, sleeping enough, managing their stress, and doing this week after week without dramatically falling off.

Why consistency beats perfection every time is the principle underneath all of this — and it's worth understanding deeply.

Addressing the Mindset, Not Just the Method

You can have the most evidence-based program in the world and still not get results if the mindset isn't addressed. The all-or-nothing thinking, the self-sabotage at the first slip, the belief that you can't trust yourself around food — these things need to be worked through, not ignored.

This is why the women I work with don't just get a training plan and a nutrition guide. They get support around the identity shifts and habit formation that make the physical changes actually stick.

You Haven't Failed. The Approach Has.

That distinction matters enormously. Because if you believe you've failed, the natural conclusion is to try harder — more restriction, more training, more discipline. And that takes you back to the same cycle.

But if the approach has failed — if the programs were always designed for short-term results rather than sustainable change — then the solution is a fundamentally different approach. Not more effort. Different effort.
