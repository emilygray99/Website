Weight Loss

Why You Keep Losing and
Regaining the Same Weight
(And How to Break the Cycle)

Emily Gray , Masters Graduate Exercise Physiologist
26 June 2025 7 min read Weight Loss

If you have lost weight before maybe even significant weight and then watched it slowly come back you aren't alone and you aren't failing. You're experiencing one of the most well-documented and frustrating patterns in exercise science: weight cycling commonly called yo-yo dieting.

The reason it happens is physiological not psychological. Understanding the mechanism is the first step to breaking it permanently.

Why does the weight come back?

When you severely restrict calories your body interprets this as a threat. It responds with a cascade of adaptations designed to keep you alive during what it perceives as a famine. These adaptations work against fat loss in the short term and against weight maintenance in the long term.

The primary mechanism is adaptive thermogenesis: a reduction in resting metabolic rate beyond what would be predicted by the loss of body mass alone. A landmark study published in Obesity (Rosenbaum et al.) found that people who had lost significant weight showed metabolic rates 10–15% lower than would be expected for their new body weight. This effect persists for years sometimes indefinitely.

Alongside this extreme restriction causes muscle loss. Muscle is metabolically expensive tissue your body burns it for fuel when calories are severely limited. Less muscle means a lower resting metabolic rate which means your body needs fewer calories to maintain itself. When you return to normal eating the surplus is greater than before.

What happens to your hormones?

Weight loss triggers hormonal changes that actively drive you toward regaining weight. Research from the New England Journal of Medicine (Sumithran et al., 2011) tracked participants for 12 months after a calorie-restricted diet and found that levels of:

  • Ghrelin (hunger hormone) increased significantly and remained elevated a year later
  • Leptin (satiety hormone) dropped and remained suppressed meaning fullness signals were chronically reduced
  • Peptide YY and cholecystokinin (appetite-suppressing hormones) both declined

The conclusion from the researchers: the hormonal response to weight loss actively favours weight regain for at least 12 months after a calorie-restricted diet ends. This isn't a willpower problem. It's a hormonal cascade your body generates automatically.

The honest truth: If you have regained weight after dieting your body was doing exactly what it was designed to do. The protocol was the problem not you.

What actually works instead?

Breaking the yo-yo cycle requires an approach that works with your physiology not against it. Based on the research and five years of coaching 450+ women here is what consistently produces lasting fat loss:

A moderate calorie deficit not an aggressive one

A deficit of 300–500 calories per day produces fat loss at approximately 0.25–0.5kg per week while minimising adaptive thermogenesis and muscle loss. This feels slower than crash dieting but the metabolic cost is dramatically lower and the results hold.

Adequate protein intake

Protein has the highest thermic effect of any macronutrient (meaning your body burns more calories digesting it) and it is essential for preserving muscle during a calorie deficit. Research supports a target of 1.6–2.2g of protein per kg of bodyweight during fat loss phases. Most women eating a moderate Australian diet are getting roughly half this amount.

Progressive strength training

Resistance training preserves and builds muscle mass which protects your metabolic rate during a calorie deficit. A 2021 meta-analysis in Obesity Reviews found that adding resistance training to a calorie deficit preserved significantly more lean mass compared to cardio or diet alone directly reducing the metabolic slowdown that causes weight regain.

Habits not heroics

The research on long-term weight maintenance consistently identifies one factor above all others: sustainable behaviour change. Not a diet you suffer through but a way of eating and training that you genuinely maintain over months and years. This requires building habits that fit your actual life not an idealised version of it.

What this looks like in practice: Training 3–4 days per week. Eating at a moderate deficit. Hitting your protein target. Sleeping adequately. Managing stress. Not perfect sustainable.

The bottom line

You're not broken. Your metabolism isn't broken. The protocols that produced short-term loss at the cost of long-term sustainability are broken. Understanding the physiological reason weight comes back adaptive thermogenesis muscle loss hormonal dysregulation removes the self-blame and points directly to a better approach.

Moderate deficit. High protein. Strength training. Consistent habits. These aren't exciting or dramatic. They are what the evidence shows actually works and they're what I build every coaching program around.

Common questions

About yo-yo dieting and weight regain

Why do I lose weight and then gain it back?+

Weight regain happens because extreme calorie restriction lowers your metabolic rate (adaptive thermogenesis) reduces muscle mass and triggers hormonal changes that increase hunger (elevated ghrelin reduced leptin). When the diet ends you return to previous eating habits but now have a slower metabolism so the weight returns often plus more.

How do I stop yo-yo dieting permanently?+

The key is a moderate calorie deficit (no more than 500 calories below maintenance) adequate protein intake (1.6–2.2g per kg of bodyweight) to preserve muscle mass and progressive strength training. This combination produces fat loss while maintaining your metabolic rate making results sustainable long term.

How much of a calorie deficit is safe for women?+

Research supports a deficit of 300–500 calories per day for sustainable fat loss of 0.25–0.5kg per week. Larger deficits accelerate short-term weight loss but significantly increase the risk of muscle loss metabolic adaptation and eventual weight regain.

Does strength training help with weight loss in women?+

Yes. A 2021 meta-analysis in Obesity Reviews found that combining resistance training with a moderate calorie deficit preserved significantly more lean muscle mass than calorie restriction alone. Preserving muscle mass maintains your metabolic rate making weight loss more sustainable and reducing likelihood of regain.
