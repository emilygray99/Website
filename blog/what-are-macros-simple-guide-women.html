What Are Macros? A Simple, No-Nonsense Guide for Women

Emily Gray , Masters Graduate Exercise Physiologist
18 May 20265 min readNutrition

If you've spent any time in fitness spaces, you've heard the word "macros" thrown around like everyone knows what it means. Track your macros. Hit your macros. Your macros are off.

But nobody actually explained it to you properly. And because nutrition is already confusing enough, you've been nodding along and hoping context would eventually make it clear.

Let's fix that today. Here's everything you need to know about macros — simply, without the jargon, and in a way that's actually useful.

What "Macros" Actually Means

"Macros" is short for macronutrients — the three main categories of nutrients your body requires in large amounts to function, perform, and recover.

Those three macronutrients are:

  • Protein
  • Carbohydrates
  • Fat

Every food you eat contains some combination of these three things. Together, they make up your total calorie intake. And how much of each you eat — not just how many calories you consume overall — has a significant impact on your body composition, energy levels, training performance, and fat loss.

Understanding macros doesn't mean you have to obsessively track every gram of food for the rest of your life. But understanding what these three nutrients do and roughly how much you need is one of the most powerful tools you have for getting results without constantly feeling confused about what to eat.

Protein: Your Most Important Macro

Protein is made up of amino acids — the building blocks your body uses to repair and build muscle tissue, produce hormones and enzymes, and support immune function.

For women focused on fat loss and body composition, protein is the most critical macro to get right. Here's why:

It preserves muscle during a calorie deficit. When you're in a calorie deficit to lose fat, your body will break down muscle for energy if protein intake is inadequate. Higher protein intake protects against this — meaning you lose fat, not muscle.

It keeps you full. Protein is the most satiating macronutrient. Adequate protein at each meal reduces hunger, makes sticking to your nutrition plan dramatically easier, and reduces the likelihood of overeating later in the day.

It supports recovery from training. Every strength training session creates micro-damage in your muscle fibres. Protein provides the raw materials your body uses to repair that damage and build back stronger.

How much protein do you need?

As an Exercise Physiologist, I recommend women focused on fat loss aim for 1.6–2.2 grams of protein per kilogram of bodyweight per day. For a 70kg woman, that's roughly 112–154 grams of protein daily.

Good protein sources: chicken, turkey, salmon, eggs, Greek yoghurt, cottage cheese, legumes, tofu, and protein supplements when whole food sources aren't convenient.

Carbohydrates: Fuel, Not the Enemy

Carbohydrates have been unfairly demonised by decades of diet culture. They are not the reason people gain weight. Excess total calories are.

Carbohydrates are your body's preferred fuel source — particularly for the brain and for exercise performance. When you eat carbohydrates, your body breaks them down into glucose, which is either used immediately for energy or stored as glycogen in your muscles and liver for later use.

For women who strength train — which should be everyone — adequate carbohydrate intake is critical for:

  • Training performance and output
  • Muscle glycogen replenishment after sessions
  • Cognitive function and mood
  • Thyroid function and hormonal health

The low-carb myth: Many women have been told to cut carbs to lose fat. And while low-carb diets can work in the short term, they're not superior to higher-carb approaches when total calories and protein are matched. The research is clear on this. What low-carb does do is make training harder, reduce muscle glycogen, and — for many women — make the approach miserable to sustain.

Eat your carbs. Just choose mostly whole food sources — rice, oats, potatoes, fruit, legumes — and time them around your training when possible.

Fat: Essential, Not Optional

Dietary fat is not the same as body fat. Eating fat does not make you fat. This myth has caused enormous harm to women's health over the past few decades.

Dietary fat is essential for:

  • Hormone production — including oestrogen, progesterone, and testosterone
  • Absorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, and K)
  • Brain health and cognitive function
  • Cell membrane integrity
  • Satiety and meal satisfaction

Women who chronically under-eat fat — which is extremely common among those who've spent years on low-fat diet approaches — often experience hormonal disruption, poor recovery, brain fog, and difficulty losing body fat despite eating in a deficit.

Good fat sources: avocado, olive oil, nuts and nut butters, fatty fish, eggs, full-fat dairy.

How Much of Each Macro Do You Need?

Here's a simple starting framework for women focused on sustainable fat loss:

Macro Target
Protein 1.6–2.2g per kg of bodyweight
Fat 0.8–1g per kg of bodyweight
Carbohydrates Fill remaining calories based on activity and preference

These are starting points, not permanent rules. Your ideal macro balance depends on your training volume, lifestyle, preferences, and how your body responds. Adjust based on how you feel, how you're recovering, and what's sustainable for you.

Do You Need to Track Macros to Get Results?

No. But understanding them helps.

Tracking macros using an app like Cronometer or MyFitnessPal can be a useful short-term awareness tool — it helps you understand portion sizes, identify where your protein might be falling short, and build a baseline understanding of what your typical meals look like nutritionally.

But tracking doesn't need to be forever. Most women I work with track for a period of time, develop a strong intuitive sense of what balanced meals look like, and then step back from daily tracking while maintaining their results.

The goal is always to understand food well enough that you don't need to obsess over it. Flexible nutrition — eating in a way that's structured but not rigid — is how that translates in practice.

The Takeaway on Macros

You don't need to make macros complicated. The foundation is straightforward:

  • Prioritise protein at every meal — it's the most important variable for fat loss and body composition
  • Don't fear carbohydrates — they fuel your training and your life
  • Include adequate fat — it's essential for hormonal health and sustainability
  • Adjust everything based on how you feel and what you can maintain
